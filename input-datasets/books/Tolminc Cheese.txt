Author: Pavlos Protopapas

# Tolminc: A Cheese, a Valley, and the Art of Waiting
*A fictional tale inspired by Alpine cheesemaking*

## Chapter One: The First Slice

The first thing Mara noticed was that nobody hurried.

She had arrived at her grandfather’s house with a suitcase, a laptop, and a carefully organized list of things she intended to accomplish during her visit. Before she could explain any of them, he placed a wooden board on the kitchen table.

On it sat bread, two apples, and a wedge of cheese.

“Tolminc,” he said.

“I know what it is.”

“Then sit down.”

Outside, the mountains above the valley were disappearing into low cloud. Inside, the kitchen smelled of coffee and the wood her grandfather had brought in that morning. He cut the cheese into uneven pieces with a knife whose handle had become smooth from years of use.

Mara tasted a piece. It was firm at first, then softer as she chewed. There was a gentle sweetness, followed by something savory that stayed after she swallowed.

“Well?” he asked.

“It is good.”

He nodded and cut himself another piece.

She had expected a speech about tradition. Her grandfather had apparently decided that the cheese could handle its own introduction.

## Chapter Two: Above the Valley

The next morning, they drove uphill before breakfast.

The road narrowed as they climbed. Houses gave way to scattered buildings, then stretches of pasture broken by trees. Mara watched the valley through the passenger window while her grandfather concentrated on the bends.

At the dairy, a woman named Anja was already working.

“You brought help,” she said.

“I brought someone who owns a laptop.”

“That may be less useful.”

Anja handed Mara an apron.

The room was warm, and the windows had clouded around the edges. There were buckets to move, surfaces to clean, and equipment whose purpose Mara could not immediately identify. She stood beside a large vat and watched Anja examine the milk.

“How do you know when it is ready?” Mara asked.

“For what?”

“For the next step.”

Anja smiled. “That is a better question.”

Over the following hours, Mara began to understand how many decisions could hide inside a process that sounded simple when described at a kitchen table. Milk became curd. The curd was cut, stirred, and handled with care. Liquid separated from solids. What had filled the vat gradually became something that could be gathered and shaped.

Anja used instruments, but she also watched closely, touched carefully, and occasionally waited when Mara thought there was nothing left to wait for.

“People like to ask for the secret,” she said. “Usually, they are disappointed when I tell them to pay attention.”

By noon, Mara’s sleeves were damp and her notebook remained unopened.

## Chapter Three: The Notebook

Her grandfather kept his records in a drawer beside the stove.

There were several notebooks, each with a different cover. Some pages contained columns of numbers. Others held brief observations, crossed-out calculations, or names followed by amounts owed.

Mara found a page with only one sentence beneath the date:

*Too warm. Turned earlier.*

“That is the whole entry?”

“It was the important part.”

Further back, the handwriting changed.

“My father,” her grandfather said.

The older entries were smaller and more crowded. Mara ran her finger beneath a line without touching the ink. She had imagined that inherited knowledge would arrive in clear instructions: a complete method, carefully preserved, explaining exactly what to do.

Instead, she found fragments.

A temperature. A change in the weather. A batch that behaved differently. A reminder to repair a shelf.

“How did you learn from this?”

“I worked beside him.”

“And the notebooks?”

“They helped me remember what to ask.”

That afternoon, Mara started a notebook of her own. On the first page, she wrote a detailed account of the morning.

Her grandfather read it and pointed to the longest paragraph.

“What did the curd feel like?”

She had not written that down.

## Chapter Four: The Room of Wheels

The aging room was cooler than the dairy.

Wheels of cheese rested on shelves in rows that looked orderly from the doorway and less identical when Mara approached them. Some were lighter, some darker. Their surfaces carried small differences she would not have noticed a week earlier.

Anja lifted one wheel, examined it, and returned it to the shelf.

“This one needs attention.”

“What is wrong with it?”

“Nothing yet.”

Mara helped turn the cheeses. The work required more strength than she expected and more care than the repeated movement suggested. After a while, she stopped counting.

She had spent much of her working life trying to shorten the distance between starting something and finishing it. Here, a day could be productive without producing anything ready to sell.

“You must hate waiting,” Anja said.

“I am learning.”

“Waiting is easy. Remembering to check is the work.”

Later, they tasted two cheeses of different ages. Mara preferred the younger one at first. It was gentler, easier to eat in another quick bite. The older cheese demanded more attention. Its flavor developed slowly and remained longer.

“Which is better?” she asked.

“What are you eating it with?”

“Nothing.”

“Then which do you want another piece of?”

Mara reached for the older cheese.

Anja pushed the board toward her.

## Chapter Five: At the Table

On Sunday, the kitchen filled with people.

A neighbor arrived carrying bread. Anja brought a wheel wrapped for the journey. Someone produced a bowl of pears, and Mara’s grandfather objected that there was already too much food while clearing space for more.

The cheese appeared in several forms over the afternoon.

First came thick slices eaten with bread. Later, Mara grated some over potatoes and watched the edges brown in the oven. At the end of the meal, a small piece remained beside half a pear on her plate.

Conversation moved between the weather, an unreliable vehicle, a family disagreement, and the question of who would take over a nearby farm. Nobody delivered a formal account of regional identity. They passed the board and argued about whether the potatoes needed more salt.

Mara thought about the descriptions she had read before coming: heritage, craftsmanship, the taste of a landscape. They were easier to write than what she was seeing.

Anja had been awake before dawn. Her grandfather’s hands hurt when the weather changed. The neighbor who brought the bread was worried about next winter.

The cheese belonged to their lives through these ordinary details.

When Mara offered to wash up, nobody refused.

## Chapter Six: What Travels Home

Before she left, her grandfather wrapped a piece of Tolminc for her.

“Not in the suitcase,” he said.

“Why?”

“You will forget it.”

She put it beside her notebook instead.

During the journey home, she opened the notebook and read what she had written. There were measurements now, but also questions. She had recorded the feel of curd, the weight of a wheel, and Anja’s habit of checking a surface twice before deciding it was clean.

Near the end, she found a sentence she had written after Sunday lunch:

*Ask who does the work.*

Back in her apartment, Mara unpacked the cheese before her clothes. She cut bread, sliced an apple, and placed everything on the table.

The kitchen was quieter than her grandfather’s. There was no stove to feed, no mountain road outside, and nobody to tell her that her slices were too thin.

She ate slowly.

The following morning, she called him.

“How is the cheese?” he asked.

“Good.”

“And the laptop?”

“Still working.”

“A pity. You could have come back.”

Mara opened her calendar.

“When do you need help?”

For the first time in their conversation, he took a while to answer.
